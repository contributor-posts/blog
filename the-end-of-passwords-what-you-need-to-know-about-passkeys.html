The End of Passwords? What You Need to Know About Passkeys

For decades, the password has been the default way to prove you are who you say you are online — and one of the weakest links in digital security. Here's how passkeys work, why they're considered phishing-resistant, and what happens if you lose the device they live on.

CP Contributor Posts Editorial Team • July 15, 2026 • 5 min read

For decades, the password has been the default way to prove you are who you say you are online. It has also been, consistently, one of the weakest links in digital security — reused across accounts, easily guessed, and constantly targeted by phishing attacks. Over the past few years, a new authentication method called the passkey has been steadily replacing it, and most people have already encountered one without realizing it.

Quick take: A passkey replaces your password with the same fingerprint, face scan, or PIN you already use to unlock your device. There's nothing to type, nothing stored on a server that can be stolen in a breach, and nothing an attacker can phish out of you.

What Exactly Is a Passkey?

A passkey is a digital credential that lets you sign into an app or website using the same method you already use to unlock your phone or laptop — a fingerprint, a face scan, or a PIN. Instead of typing a password that a server has to store and protect, your device creates a matched pair of cryptographic keys: a public key that lives on the company's server, and a private key that never leaves your device.

When you log in, the website sends a small challenge to your device. Your device uses the private key to respond, proving it's really you, without ever transmitting a secret over the internet. There is nothing typed, nothing stored on a server that a hacker could steal in a breach, and nothing to reuse across sites.

This approach is built on open standards developed by the FIDO Alliance, an industry consortium whose members include Google, Apple, Microsoft, and hundreds of other technology and financial companies.

Why Passwords Are So Hard to Secure

Passwords fail in predictable ways. People reuse them across multiple accounts, choose ones that are easy to guess, and are routinely tricked into typing them into fake login pages. Even a "strong" password can be rendered useless the moment it is phished or leaked in a data breach, because the same string of characters can be replayed anywhere it was used.

Security researchers have argued for years that the real fix isn't stronger passwords — it's removing passwords from the equation entirely. That is the philosophy behind guidance from bodies like the U.S. National Institute of Standards and Technology, which has increasingly pushed organizations toward phishing-resistant authentication methods rather than relying on password complexity rules alone.

How Passkeys Solve the Problem

Passkeys are considered phishing-resistant because there is no shared secret for an attacker to intercept or trick you into revealing. A fake login page can ask you for a passkey, but it has no way to actually use one, since the private key is cryptographically tied to the legitimate website's domain. If you're not on the real site, the passkey simply won't work.

They're also more convenient. There's nothing to remember, nothing to type, and no password manager required just to keep track of dozens of unique logins. Signing in becomes as fast as unlocking your phone.

Most major platforms already support them. Apple, Google, and Microsoft all offer passkey creation and sync across their ecosystems, and large services — from Amazon to PayPal to GitHub — have added passkey sign-in options. Independent research from the FIDO Alliance has tracked adoption climbing steadily as awareness grows.

What About Losing Your Device?

This is the most common concern people raise, and it's a fair one. If your private key lives only on your phone, what happens if you lose the phone?

In practice, most consumer passkey implementations solve this through secure syncing. Apple's iCloud Keychain, Google Password Manager, and similar services sync your passkeys across your devices using end-to-end encryption, so a lost phone doesn't mean a lost account. For higher-security use cases, some organizations instead use device-bound passkeys stored on hardware security keys, which trade a bit of convenience for an extra layer of assurance. Google's developer documentation on passkeys breaks down these tradeoffs and how the underlying protections actually work.

Is This the End of the Password Era?

Not overnight. Plenty of older systems, internal business tools, and smaller websites still rely on traditional logins, and full migration takes time. But the direction is clear: major platforms are pushing users toward passkeys by default, browsers now support the underlying WebAuthn standard natively, and the security case against passwords keeps getting stronger with every large-scale data breach.

For most people, the practical takeaway is simple. The next time an app or website offers to set up a passkey, it's worth doing — it's one of the few security upgrades that makes you both safer and faster at the same time.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I still need a password manager if I use passkeys?

For now, yes — most people still have accounts that only support passwords. Many password managers also store and sync passkeys, so the two can coexist during the transition.

Can a passkey be phished like a password?

No. A passkey is cryptographically tied to the real website's domain, so it simply won't function on a lookalike phishing site — there's no secret to type in that a fake page could capture.

What happens if I lose the device my passkey is on?

If your passkeys sync through iCloud Keychain, Google Password Manager, or a similar service, you can recover them on a new device. Device-bound passkeys tied to a single hardware key don't sync, so it's worth registering a backup method for those accounts.

Bottom line
Passkeys over passwords

Faster to use, nothing to remember, and resistant to phishing — worth switching to wherever it's offered.
